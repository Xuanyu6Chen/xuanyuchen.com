A healthy economy requires that the financial system perform the essential function of channeling funds from those who save to those who have a shortage of funds. But how do we ensure efficiency and fairness? Let's analyze the design of the financial structure. 

Companies across the world finance their activities using external funds. Those funds come from bank loans, nonbank loans, bonds, and stocks. 

1. **Financial markets and intermediaries are heavily regulated**. 

2. **Large, established corporations generally have easier access to public securities markets**. 

3. **Collateral and borrower net worth matter in debt financing**. 
*Collateral* is property that is pledged to a lender to guarantee payment in the event that the borrower is unable to make debt payments. 

4. **Debt contracts commonly contain restrictive covenants**. 
Bonds and loan contracts typically are long legal documents with *provisions called restrictive covenants that restrict and specify* certain activities that the borrower can engage in. 

5. **Financial intermediaries remain important**, although their roles and relative importance vary across countries and over time. 

Transaction costs and asymmetric information help explain why the five facts above hold. 

## Transaction Costs 
Transaction costs are a major problem in financial markets. 

Most people only have a small amount of funds available, so people can only make a restricted number of investments or pay large transaction costs for many small transactions. 

One solution to the problem is to **bundle the funds of many investors together** so that they can take advantage of *economies of scale*. The clearest example of a financial intermediary that arose because of economies of scale is the **mutual fund**. 

In general, financial intermediaries develop expertise and use technology to lower transaction costs so that it is easier for us to conduct transactions. 

## Asymmetric Information 
Asymmetric information is a situation that arises when one party's insufficient knowledge about the other party involved in a transaction *makes it impossible to make accurate decisions when conducting the transaction*. Asymmetric information can cause a serious problem for the efficiency of financial markets. 

**Agency theory** explains how asymmetric information problems affect economic behavior. 

### Adverse Selection 
*Adverse selection* is an asymmetric information problem that occurs *before the transaction*. Potential bad credit risks are the ones who most actively seek out loans. 
 
The **lemons problem** from Nobel Prize winner George Akerlof explains adverse selection. 

In the used-car market, potential buyers are frequently unable to assess the quality of the car. The price that a buyer pays must reflect the average quality of the cars in the market. Therefore, the owner of a bad-quality car, the lemon, is more than happy to sell it at the average price. But the owner of a good-quality car, the peach, is unwilling to sell at a price below its value. As a result of this adverse selection, few good used cars will come to the market. 

A similar lemons problem arises in securities markets. Lacking information, investors cannot distinguish good firms with high expected profits and low risk from bad firms with low expected profits and high risk. 

#### Tools to Help Solve Adverse Selection Problems 
In the absence of asymmetric information, the lemons problem goes away. There are several ways to reduce asymmetric information. 

##### Sale of Information 
The solution to the adverse selection problem in financial markets is to reduce asymmetric information. One way is for companies like Standard & Poor's to gather information and sell it to subscribers. 

However, this doesn't completely solve the adverse selection problem because of the **free-rider problem**. It occurs when people who do not pay for the information take advantage of the information that other people paid for. 

##### Government Regulation 
The government regulates securities markets in a way that encourages firms to reveal honest information. In the United States, the SEC is the government agency that requires firms selling their securities to have **independent audits**. 

##### Financial Intermediation 
In the used-car market, most used cars are not sold directly by one individual to another but through **dealers**. Used-car dealers produce information in the market by becoming experts in determining whether a car is a peach or a lemon. 

Similarly, financial intermediaries, such as banks, play an important role by **becoming experts in producing information that separates good credit risks from bad ones**. 

An important element in a bank's ability to profit is that it avoids the free-rider problem by primarily **making private loans** that are nontradable in the open market. 

Another important fact is that when the quality of information about firms is better, asymmetric information problems will be less severe, and it will be easier for firms to issue securities. That is why the better known a corporation is, the easier it is to raise money in the open market. 

##### Collateral and Net Worth 
**Collateral** reduces the consequences of adverse selection because it reduces the lender's losses in the event of a default. 

**Net worth** (the difference between a firm's assets and its liabilities) can perform a similar role to that of collateral. 

### Moral Hazard 
*Moral hazard* arises *after the transaction occurs*. The lender runs the risk that the borrower will engage in activities that are undesirable from the lender's point of view. 

#### In Equity Markets
The **principal-agent problem** is moral hazard in equity markets, and it comes from the **separation of ownership and control**.

Stockholders (the principals) own the firm, but managers (the agents) run it, and managers know more about what they're doing than stockholders do. Because managers usually own only a small share of the firm, they keep only a little of the profit they create. So they may act in their own interest, or even hide or misreport profits, and stockholders can't easily see it. 

Several tools can help solve the principal-agent problem. 

##### Monitoring 
One way is to engage in **monitoring of the firm's activities**. The problem is that monitoring is **costly and invites free riding**. 

##### Government Regulation 
Governments have laws to force firms to adhere to standard accounting principles that make profit verification easier. 

##### Financial Intermediation 
One financial intermediary that helps reduce this moral hazard is the **venture capital firm**. Venture firms usually insist on having several of their own people participate as members of the managing body, so they can keep a close watch on the firm's activities. 

#### In Debt Markets
A debt contract requires the borrower to pay out a fixed amount and lets the borrower keep any profits above that amount. Therefore, borrowers have an **incentive to take on risky investment** projects. 

##### Net Worth and Collateral 
When borrowers have more at stake because of their net worth or valuable collateral, the risk of moral hazard will be greatly reduced. 

The greater the borrower's net worth and pledged collateral, the greater the borrower's incentive to behave in the way that the lender expects and desires, and the smaller the moral hazard problem in the debt contract. 

##### Monitoring and Enforcement of Restrictive Covenants 
Lenders can **write provisions** (restrictive covenants) into the debt contract to make sure the borrower uses the money the way the lender wants. 

##### Financial Intermediation 
Although restrictive covenants help reduce the moral hazard problem, they do not completely eliminate it. Monitoring and enforcing the restrictive covenants are costly, and free-rider problems can arise. 

Financial intermediaries have the ability to avoid the free-rider problem as long as **they make primarily private loans**. Private loans are not traded, so no one else can free-ride on the intermediary's monitoring and enforcement of the restrictive covenants.
