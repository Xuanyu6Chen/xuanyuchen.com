We use money every day. To economists, money has a very specific meaning. *Money is anything that is accepted in payment for goods or services or in the repayment of debts*. Currency is one type of money.

## Functions of Money 
Any type or form of money should have three primary functions in any economy: as a medium of exchange, as a unit of account, and as a store of value. 

### Medium of Exchange 
Money in the *form of currency or checks* is a **medium of exchange**. The use of money as a medium of exchange promotes economic efficiency. 

Money reduces transaction costs compared with a barter economy. 

### Unit of Account 
A **unit of account** is used to *measure value in the economy*. 

### Store of Value 
A **store of value** is *a repository of purchasing power over time*. How good a store of value money is depends on the price level. During inflation, money loses value rapidly as the price level rises fast. 

## Evolution of the Payments System 
The payments system has been evolving over centuries: from *commodity money*, to *fiat money*, to *checks*, to *electronic payment*, and finally to *e-money*. 

The most important step in this evolution is the move to fiat money. 

From the 1870s to 1914, major currencies were on the **classical gold standard**, under which they could be exchanged for a fixed weight of gold. In 1944, near the end of World War II, the **Bretton Woods system** was established: the dollar stayed tied to gold, and every other currency was pegged to the dollar. In 1971, President Nixon ended the dollar's link to gold. By 1973 the major **currencies were floating**, and **money is now fiat** (paper currency decreed by the government as legal tender). 

## Measuring Money 
To measure money, we need a precise definition of exactly which assets should be included. 

The Fed has settled on the following measures of the money supply, called *monetary aggregates*. 

The narrowest measure of money is M1. It includes the most liquid assets: **currency, checking account deposits, and savings deposits** (including money market deposit accounts). 

The M2 monetary aggregate adds other assets that are not quite as liquid as those in M1. Those assets are **small-denomination time deposits** (a type of CD, under \$100,000) and **retail money market mutual fund shares**. 

Savings deposits counted only in M2 until May 2020, when the Fed moved them into M1.
