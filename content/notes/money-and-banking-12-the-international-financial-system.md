The economies of the entire world are growing more and more interdependent. Let's examine how the international financial system affects monetary policy. 

## Intervention in the Foreign Exchange Market 

In [[money-and-banking-11-the-foreign-exchange-market|The Foreign Exchange Market]], we analyzed the market as a completely free market. However, the foreign exchange market is not free of government intervention. **Central banks regularly engage in foreign exchange market transactions to influence exchange rates.** 

Suppose that the Fed decides to sell \$1 billion of its foreign assets in exchange for \$1 billion of U.S. currency. This reduces the Fed's holdings of international reserves by \$1 billion, and currency in circulation or reserves fall by \$1 billion. The monetary base, which equals currency in circulation plus reserves, has fallen by \$1 billion. 

Because $M = m \times MB$ ([[money-and-banking-8-the-money-supply-process#The Money Multiplier Formula|The Money Multiplier Formula]]), a smaller monetary base means a smaller money supply.

A smaller money supply raises $i_D$ ([[money-and-banking-4-the-behavior-of-interest-rates#Shifts in the Supply of Money|Shifts in the Supply of Money]]).

A higher $i_D$ raises $R^D$, so demand for dollar assets shifts right and the dollar appreciates ([[money-and-banking-11-the-foreign-exchange-market#Domestic Interest Rate|Domestic Interest Rate]]).

$$MB \downarrow \;\Rightarrow\; M \downarrow \;\Rightarrow\; i_D \uparrow \;\Rightarrow\; R^D \uparrow \;\Rightarrow\; E_t \uparrow$$

### Unsterilized Intervention 
The intervention we talked about above, in which a central bank allows the purchase or sale of domestic currency to **have an effect on the monetary base**, is called an *unsterilized foreign exchange intervention*. 

### Sterilized Intervention 
However, if the central bank does not want the purchase or sale of domestic currency to affect the monetary base, **it can conduct an open market purchase/sale to offset the decrease/increase in the monetary base**. This process is called *sterilized foreign exchange intervention*. 

## Balance of Payments
The *balance of payments* is a *record of every transaction between a country's residents and the rest of the world over a period*.

![Balance of payments](/images/money-and-banking/bop-us-balance-of-payments-2025.png)
*Balance of Payments*

The balance of payments has two accounts:
- *Current account ($CA$)*: trade in goods and services, investment income, and transfers.
	- *Goods balance* = **exports of goods − imports of goods**. In 2025 the U.S. goods balance was −\$1,260 billion (a goods deficit).
	- *Services balance* works the same way for services. In 2025 it was +\$328 billion (a services surplus).
- *Financial account ($FA$):* capital inflows minus capital outflows (foreigners buying domestic assets minus residents buying foreign assets).

Whatever the two accounts leave over is settled by the central bank's international reserves:
$$CA + FA = \Delta \text{official reserves}$$

> [!example] A country runs a current account deficit of \$800 billion ($CA = -800$) and has net capital inflows of \$790 billion ($FA = +790$). Then $\Delta \text{official reserves} = -800 + 790 = -10$: the central bank's international reserves fall by \$10 billion.

## Exchange Rate Regimes
As we have mentioned in [[money-and-banking-2-what-is-money#Evolution of the Payments System|Evolution of the Payments System]], exchange rate regimes have moved from fixed to floating: from the *gold standard*, to the *Bretton Woods system*, to today's floating rates. 

Let's go deeper into how the fixed regime worked and why it failed. 

### How a Fixed Exchange Rate Regime Works 
In a fixed exchange rate regime, *a country pegs its currency to an anchor currency at a par value, $E_{par}$, and promises to keep it there*. The market still has its own equilibrium exchange rate, $E^*$, set by supply and demand ([[money-and-banking-11-the-foreign-exchange-market#Exchange Rates in the Short Run|Exchange Rates in the Short Run]]). 

**Whenever $E^*$ differs from $E_{par}$, the central bank fills the gap by buying or selling its own currency with its international reserves**, which is an unsterilized intervention (see *Unsterilized Intervention* above).

![How a fixed exchange rate regime works](/images/money-and-banking/fx-fixed-exchange-rate.png)
*How a Fixed Exchange Rate Regime Works*

*Overvalued currency, panel (a).* When the par value is above the equilibrium, the central bank buys its own currency and sells international reserves. However, **reserves are limited, so this cannot go on forever.** 

*Undervalued currency, panel (b).* When the par value is below the equilibrium, the central bank sells its own currency and buys international reserves, which increases the monetary base. **Reserves never run out here, but the growing monetary base causes inflation.** 

The *Bretton Woods system* was set up in 1944. The U.S. dollar was tied to gold at \$35 per ounce, and every other currency was pegged to the dollar, so **the dollar became the anchor currency of the world**.

**The system worked mainly because of the strength of the U.S. economy.** After the war, the United States produced about half of the world's output and held most of the world's gold, so its promise to exchange dollars for gold was believable. Other countries also needed dollars to rebuild and to buy American goods.

### How a Fixed Exchange Rate Regime Failed
In the 1960s, the United States began running persistent balance of payments deficits: spending on the Vietnam War and on social programs, together with U.S. investment abroad, sent more dollars out of the country than came back in. 

To keep their pegs, foreign central banks had to buy these dollars, so **dollars piled up abroad as other countries' international reserves**. By the late 1960s, foreign central banks held more dollars than the United States had gold, so **the promise to exchange dollars for gold at \$35 per ounce was no longer believable**. 

The U.S. had inflation because of its own policy and the war, so the dollar became overvalued and other currencies became undervalued. To hold their pegs, **foreign central banks had to keep buying dollars by creating more of their own currency, which led to inflation imported from the U.S.** (panel (b) above). Countries such as West Germany became unwilling to keep doing this. In August 1971, President Nixon stopped exchanging dollars for gold. 

**Bretton Woods failed because persistent U.S. deficits left too many dollars abroad and too little gold behind them, and because holding the pegs forced other countries to import U.S. inflation.**

### The Policy Trilemma 
As we can see, a country that ties its exchange rate to the anchor currency of a larger country **loses control of its monetary policy**. 

A country can't pursue the following three policies at the same time: (1) **free capital mobility**, (2) **a fixed exchange rate**, and (3) **an independent monetary policy**. 

## International Considerations and Monetary Policy
Monetary policy can be affected by international matters in the following ways. 

### Monetary Base
When a central bank intervenes in the foreign exchange market, it gives up some control of its monetary policy by changing the monetary base, unless it sterilizes the intervention.

### Balance of Payments 
Balance of payments considerations were far more important under the Bretton Woods system. When a country **runs a balance of payments deficit**, it **loses international reserves and is forced to tighten monetary policy to protect its peg**.

### Exchange Rates
**Central banks also care about the value of their currency.** Central banks can conduct contractionary or expansionary monetary policy to appreciate or depreciate their currency. 

## To Peg or Not to Peg
Even though most major currencies now float, some countries still choose to peg their currency to an anchor currency. This is called *exchange-rate targeting*.

Advantages. **A peg gives the country a nominal anchor**: its inflation is tied to the anchor country's, which helps a country **with a history of high inflation**. It is also an automatic rule for monetary policy, and it is simple and clear.

Disadvantages. **The country loses its independent monetary policy**: shocks to the anchor country are passed on, and the country cannot respond to its own recessions. **The peg is open to speculative attacks**: once markets doubt it, they sell the currency until the peg breaks. 

> [!example] *United Kingdom, 1992.* The pound was pegged to the German mark. When Germany raised interest rates, the UK had to keep its rates high during a recession. Speculators, including George Soros, bet that the UK would not keep paying that price, and the pound left the peg on September 16, 1992.

> [!example] *Thailand, 1997.* The baht was pegged to the dollar, while Thai banks and firms had borrowed heavily in dollars. A speculative attack used up the central bank's reserves, the baht was floated in July 1997 and lost about half its value, and the dollar debts set off a banking crisis.

### Currency Boards 
A *currency board* is a system in which the domestic currency is backed 100% by a foreign currency, and the central bank establishes a fixed exchange rate to this foreign currency and stands ready to exchange domestic currency for the foreign currency at this rate whenever the public requests it. 

> [!example] *Hong Kong, 1997–98.* Hong Kong uses a *currency board*: every Hong Kong dollar in the monetary base is backed by U.S. dollar reserves, so it cannot run out of dollars to defend the peg. When speculators attacked, interest rates rose sharply instead, and **the peg held**, at the cost of high rates and a property slump.
