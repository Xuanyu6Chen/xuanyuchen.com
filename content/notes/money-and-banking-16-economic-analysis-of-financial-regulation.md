The financial system is among the most heavily regulated sectors of the economy, and banks are among the most heavily regulated of financial institutions. 

Let's develop an economic analysis of why regulation of the financial system takes the form it does. 

## Financial Regulation 
The concepts of asymmetric information, adverse selection, and moral hazard are especially useful in understanding regulations. They explain the several basic categories of financial regulation below. 

### Government Safety Net 

#### FDIC Insurance 
Before the FDIC started operations in 1934, depositors of a failed bank had to wait until the bank was liquidated to get their funds back, and often received only part of them. Because depositors lacked information about how well a bank managed its risk, they rushed to withdraw their money whenever they feared a bank might fail, and these runs could spread into a full bank panic. 

**A government safety net for depositors can short-circuit runs on banks and bank panics**. One form is deposit insurance, a guarantee such as that provided by the *Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC)* in the U.S., under which depositors are currently paid in full on the first \$250,000 they have deposited in a bank if the bank fails. 

The FDIC uses two primary methods to handle a failed bank. One is the *payoff method*, in which the FDIC allows the bank to fail and pays off deposits up to the \$250,000 insurance limit. The FDIC then takes over the insured depositors' claims and, as the bank's assets are sold, is paid alongside uninsured depositors in proportion to their claims, ahead of general creditors and shareholders. 

The second is the *purchase and assumption method*, in which the FDIC reorganizes the bank **by finding a willing merger partner to take over the failed bank's deposits and other liabilities**. The FDIC often sweetens the pot for the merger partner by providing it with subsidized loans or by buying some of the failed bank's weaker loans. It is typically more costly for the FDIC than the payoff method, but it is the more common procedure. 

#### Lending 
Another way governments provide support is through **lending from the central bank** to troubled institutions, as the Fed did during the 2008 financial crisis. This form is often referred to as the *"lender of last resort" role of the central bank*. 

#### Moral Hazard and Adverse Selection 
Although a government safety net can help protect depositors and other creditors and prevent financial crises, **moral hazard and adverse selection are the main concerns**. 

With the government safety net, financial institutions have an incentive to take on greater risks than they otherwise would -- **"Heads I win, tails the taxpayer loses"**. 

The safety net also creates adverse selection. Because depositors are protected and have little reason to monitor banks, **risk-loving entrepreneurs, and even crooks, are the people most attracted to running a bank**. 

### Restrictions on Asset Holdings 
Even without the government safety net, financial institutions still have an incentive to take on too much risk. Risky assets may provide the financial institution with higher earnings when they pay off, but **if they do not pay off and the institution fails, depositors and creditors are left holding the bag**, not the bank itself. 

Monitoring is therefore necessary, but it is very difficult for depositors and creditors to do it themselves. Thus, banks are **subject to strict regulations that restrict their holdings of risky assets**, like common stocks, and **promote diversification**. 

### Capital Requirements 
Similarly, government-imposed capital requirements are another way of minimizing moral hazard. **When a bank holds more capital, it has more to lose if it fails**, so it has less incentive to take on too much risk. The requirements take two forms. 

The first is based on the *leverage ratio*: 

$$\text{Leverage Ratio} = \frac{\text{Capital}}{\text{Total Assets}}$$

A bank is *well capitalized* if its leverage ratio exceeds 5%, and **a ratio below 3% triggers increased regulatory restrictions**. 

The second is *risk-based capital requirements* under the *Basel Accord*, which also account for riskier assets and off-balance-sheet activities: 

$$\frac{\text{Capital}}{\text{Risk-Weighted Assets}} \geq 8\%$$

Riskier assets get higher weights (e.g., 0% for government securities, 100% for business loans), so **a riskier bank must hold more capital**.

### Prompt Corrective Action 
If a bank's capital falls too low, it has little left to lose and an incentive to take on even more risk. To stop this, the *FDIC Improvement Act of 1991 (FDICIA)* requires the FDIC to intervene earlier and more vigorously when a bank gets into trouble. 

Banks are sorted into five groups by capital, from *well capitalized* to *critically undercapitalized*. Undercapitalized banks must submit a plan to restore capital, restrict their asset growth, and get approval before opening new branches. **A critically undercapitalized bank, with an equity-to-asset ratio of 2% or less, must be closed and put into receivership**, usually within 90 days.

### Financial Supervision: Chartering and Examination 
*Financial supervision* (overseeing who operates financial institutions and how they are operated) is an important way to reduce adverse selection and moral hazard. 

#### Chartering 
*Chartering* is the process of screening proposals to open new financial institutions, and it **reduces adverse selection by keeping risk-loving entrepreneurs and crooks out of the industry**. 

To get a charter, applicants must show how they plan to run the bank, the quality of the proposed management, and how much capital they will start with. National banks are chartered by the *Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC)*, and state banks by their *state banking authority*. Once a bank has been chartered, it is required to file periodic call reports. 

The difference between the two charters is **who regulates the bank and which rules it follows, not where it can operate**. A *nationally chartered* bank (marked "N.A.", like JPMorgan Chase Bank, N.A.) is regulated by the OCC under federal banking law. A *state-chartered* bank is regulated by its state and by the FDIC (or by the Fed, if it is a Fed member), and its powers are set by state law. Since the Riegle-Neal Act (1994) and Dodd-Frank (2010), both can open branches in other states. 

#### Examination 
Bank examinations **reduce moral hazard by monitoring whether a bank is following the rules** after it opens. Examiners make regular on-site visits and give the bank a *CAMELS rating*, based on Capital adequacy, Asset quality, Management, Earnings, Liquidity, and Sensitivity to market risk. 

### Assessment of Risk Management 
A bank's balance sheet only shows its risk at one point in time, and with modern trading a bank can take on huge risk very quickly. So examiners now also assess **whether a bank's risk management can keep its risk under control**. 

One key tool is the *stress test*, which calculates a bank's losses and capital under a hypothetical severe scenario, such as a deep recession with a stock market crash and soaring unemployment. 

### Disclosure Requirements 
Because of the free-rider problem, depositors and investors will not produce enough information about a bank's risk on their own. So the *Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)* requires publicly traded corporations, including bank holding companies, to **disclose information about their finances and risks** using standard accounting principles.

### Consumer Protection 
Consumers also face asymmetric information, since lenders know the true cost of a loan better than borrowers do. The *Truth in Lending Act*, part of the *Consumer Credit Protection Act* passed in 1968 and in effect since 1969, requires all lenders to **disclose the cost of borrowing**. This lets borrowers **compare loans on the same basis**. 

### Restrictions on Competition 
Increased competition can also increase moral hazard, since **banks with falling profits have an incentive to take on more risk** to keep earning. So governments once restricted competition. 

However, **most of these restrictions have been removed**, because they led to higher charges for consumers and less efficient banks. 

### Macroprudential Versus Microprudential Supervision
*Microprudential supervision* focuses on **the safety and soundness of each individual financial institution**. It looks at each bank's own capital, assets and risk management, without considering how the bank's actions affect the rest of the financial system. 

On the other hand, *macroprudential supervision* focuses on **the safety and soundness of the financial system as a whole**. Even if each bank looks well capitalized on its own, banks can all cut lending and sell assets at the same time when asset prices fall, which drives prices down further and spreads the trouble across the system. 

## The Dodd-Frank Bill 
After the 2008 financial crisis, Congress passed the *Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act* in 2010, the most comprehensive financial reform since the Great Depression. 

### Consumer Protection 
Dodd-Frank created the *Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB)*, which **writes and enforces rules for consumer financial products** such as mortgages and credit cards. 

### Resolution Authority 
The government gained the authority to **take over and wind down large failing financial firms**, not just banks, in an orderly way that avoids a disorderly collapse like Lehman Brothers. 

### Systemic Risk Regulation 
The *Financial Stability Oversight Council (FSOC)* can designate nonbank financial firms as *systemically important*, and these firms, like the largest banks, face **Fed supervision, stress tests and higher capital requirements**. 

### Volcker Rule 
Banks are **limited in proprietary trading** (trading securities with their own money) and in **investing in hedge funds and private equity funds**. 

### Derivatives 
Standardized derivatives must be **traded on exchanges and cleared through clearinghouses**, so their risks are more transparent and a single firm's failure is less likely to spread. 

### Deposit Insurance 
Dodd-Frank **permanently raised the deposit insurance limit from \$100,000 to \$250,000**, an increase first made temporarily during the 2008 crisis.
