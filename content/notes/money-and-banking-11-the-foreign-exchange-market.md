The price of one currency in terms of another is called the *exchange rate*. We begin the study of international finance by examining the foreign exchange market. 

## Foreign Exchange Market 
There are two kinds of exchange rate transactions. The predominant kind is *spot transactions*, which involve the immediate (two-day) exchange of bank deposits. *Forward transactions* involve the exchange of bank deposits at some specified future date. 

**When a currency increases in value, it experiences appreciation; when it falls in value and is worth less, it undergoes depreciation.** 

We have talked about the [[money-and-banking-2-what-is-money#Evolution of the Payments System|History of money]], in which we mentioned the exchange-rate systems used then and now. But why are exchange rates so important? **Exchange rates affect the relative price of domestic and foreign goods.** 

When a country's currency appreciates, **the country's goods abroad become more expensive and foreign goods in that country become cheaper**. Conversely, when a country's currency depreciates, its goods abroad become cheaper and foreign goods in that country become more expensive. 

### How Is Foreign Exchange Traded? 
Unlike stocks, which trade in a centralized location, *currencies are traded on the Over-the-Counter (OTC) market in which several hundred dealers (mostly banks) stand ready to buy and sell deposits denominated in foreign currencies*. 

About **$9.6 trillion** is traded every day in the OTC foreign exchange market, and the US dollar is on one side of **89%** of those trades (BIS Triennial Survey, April 2025).

Most of the volume comes from finance: banks and investors such as asset managers, pension funds, and hedge funds buy foreign securities, hedge them, and roll those hedges over.

## Exchange Rates in the Long Run
Exchange rates, just like the price of any good or asset, are determined by the interaction of supply and demand. 

### Law of One Price 
The starting point for understanding how exchange rates are determined is an idea called the *law of one price*. If two countries **produce an identical good**, and transportation costs and trade barriers are very low, **the price of the good should be the same** throughout the world no matter which country produces it. 

> [!example] Both the United States and Japan produce apples. American apples cost \$2 per lb, and Japanese apples cost ¥200 per lb. Given the law of one price, the exchange rate between the yen and the dollar must be ¥100 per dollar.

### Theory of Purchasing Power Parity 
The theory of purchasing power parity (PPP) is one of the most prominent theories of how exchange rates are determined. **It states that exchange rates between any two currencies will adjust to reflect changes in the price levels of the two countries.** It is an application of the law of one price to national price levels rather than to individual prices. 

**The theory of PPP suggests that if one country's price level rises relative to another's, its currency should depreciate.** 

Relying solely on the theory of PPP is not enough because it assumes that *all goods are identical in both countries* and that *transportation costs and trade barriers are very low*. In real life, this is not the case most of the time. In addition, PPP theory *does not take into account that many goods and services, such as housing and land, are not traded across borders*. 

### Factors That Affect Exchange Rates in the Long Run
If **a factor increases the demand for domestic goods relative to foreign goods**, the domestic currency will appreciate; if a factor decreases the relative demand for domestic goods, the domestic currency will depreciate.

#### Relative Price Levels 
In the long run, **a rise in a country's relative price level causes its currency to depreciate and a fall in its relative price level causes its currency to appreciate.** 

#### Trade Barriers 
Tariffs and quotas can affect the exchange rate. **An increase in trade barriers causes the demand for domestic goods to increase**. **Increasing trade barriers causes a country's currency to appreciate in the long run.** 

#### Preferences for Domestic Versus Foreign Goods 
**Increased demand for a country's exports causes its currency to appreciate in the long run; conversely, increased demand for imports causes the domestic currency to depreciate.** 

#### Productivity 
**In the long run, as a country becomes more productive relative to other countries, its currency appreciates.** 

## Exchange Rates in the Short Run 
Unlike in the long run, exchange rates are much more volatile in the short run. We choose to investigate short-run exchange rates with **a supply and demand analysis.** 

Supply Curve -- the quantity of dollar assets supplied is primarily the quantity of bank deposits, bonds, and equities in the U.S., so **we can take the amount as fixed with respect to the exchange rate**. 

Demand Curve -- people hold dollar assets when their expected return beats the expected return on foreign assets, where the *expected return on dollar assets (in terms of foreign currency) equals the dollar interest rate plus the expected appreciation of the dollar*:

$$R^D = i_D + \frac{E^e_{t+1} - E_t}{E_t}$$
> [!note] $R^D$ is not the exchange rate. It is the expected return on dollar assets: a change in $R^D$ changes the demand for dollar assets, and that change in demand moves the exchange rate $E_t$.

Holding $i_D$, $i_F$, and the expected future exchange rate $E^e_{t+1}$ constant, **a lower exchange rate today means a larger expected appreciation of the dollar, so the expected return on dollar assets rises and the quantity of dollar assets demanded increases.** That is why the demand curve slopes downward.

### Shifts in Demand 

#### Domestic Interest Rate 
As we can see from the formula, when the domestic interest rate $i_D$ rises, the return on dollar assets increases relative to the return on foreign assets, so people will want to hold more dollar assets, ceteris paribus. 

**An increase in the domestic interest rate $i_D$ shifts the demand curve for domestic assets, D, to the right and causes the domestic currency to appreciate.** 

#### Foreign Interest Rate 
**An increase in the foreign interest rate $i_F$ shifts the demand curve D to the left and causes the domestic currency to depreciate.** 

#### Changes in the Expected Future Exchange Rate 
Any factor that causes the expected future exchange rate, $E^e_{t+1}$, to rise increases the expected appreciation of the currency. 

Therefore, **a rise in the expected future exchange rate, $E^e_{t+1}$, shifts the demand curve to the right and causes an appreciation of the domestic currency.** 

### Real Interest Rate 
The Fisher equation in [[money-and-banking-3-understanding-interest-rates#The Fisher Equation|Real & Nominal Interest Rates]] states that the nominal interest rate equals the real interest rate plus expected inflation: 
$$i = i_r + \pi^e$$

Thus, we know that the domestic interest rate, $i_D$, can change for two reasons: either the real interest rate changes or the expected inflation rate changes. 

1) If the **domestic real interest rate increases**, we can assume the expected appreciation is unchanged due to the unchanged expected inflation. According to the formula above, the expected return on dollar assets, $R^D$, rises, so the demand curve shifts to the right and **the domestic currency appreciates**. 
2) If **expected inflation increases**, we will get a different result since it leads to a decline in the expected appreciation of the currency. As a result, the relative expected return falls, the demand curve shifts to the left, and **the exchange rate falls**.

### Summary

| Factor | Change | Quantity of dollar assets demanded at each $E_t$ | Response of $E_t$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Domestic interest rate, $i_D$ | ↑ | ↑ (D shifts right) | ↑ appreciates |
| Foreign interest rate, $i_F$ | ↑ | ↓ (D shifts left) | ↓ depreciates |
| Expected domestic price level, $P^e$ | ↑ | ↓ (D shifts left) | ↓ depreciates |
| Expected tariffs and quotas | ↑ | ↑ (D shifts right) | ↑ appreciates |
| Expected import demand | ↑ | ↓ (D shifts left) | ↓ depreciates |
| Expected export demand | ↑ | ↑ (D shifts right) | ↑ appreciates |
| Expected productivity | ↑ | ↑ (D shifts right) | ↑ appreciates |
