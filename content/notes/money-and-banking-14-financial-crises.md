A **financial crisis** occurs when information flows in financial markets experience a particularly large disruption: **financial frictions** increase and financial markets stop functioning. 

## Dynamics of Financial Crises in Advanced Economies 
Financial crises in advanced economies have progressed in two and sometimes three stages. 

![Sequence of events in financial crises in advanced economies](/images/money-and-banking/fc-stages-of-a-financial-crisis.png)
*Sequence of Events in Financial Crises in Advanced Economies*

### Stage One: Initiation of Financial Crisis 

#### Mismanagement of Financial Innovation/Liberalization 
The first path of Stage One is when the economy introduces new types of loans, known as *financial innovation*, or when countries engage in *financial liberalization*, the elimination of restrictions on financial markets and institutions. 

In the short run, those can prompt financial institutions to go on a lending spree, called a *credit boom*. However, lenders may not have the expertise or the incentives to **manage the risk appropriately** in these new lines of business. 

Government safety nets weaken market discipline and increase the moral hazard incentive for banks to take on greater risk. **Financial institutions can make risky and high-interest loans to borrowers**, walking away with nice profits if the loans are repaid, and relying on government deposit insurance if borrowers default. 

Eventually, **the value of the loans falls relative to liabilities** (loans are on the asset side, so losses reduce assets), driving down the net worth of banks and other financial institutions. With less capital, banks cut back on lending, and borrowers decrease their spending, causing economic activity to contract. 

#### Asset-Price Boom and Bust 
Prices of assets like equity shares and real estate can be well above the values based on realistic expectations, called *fundamental economic values*. A rise of asset prices above the fundamental economic values is an *asset-price bubble*. 

Asset-price bubbles are often driven by credit booms. When the bubble bursts and asset prices realign with fundamental economic values, companies' net worth declines. These companies are more likely to **make riskier investments because they have less to lose** (moral hazard problem), causing financial institutions to tighten lending standards. 

### Stage Two: Banking Crisis 
Deteriorating balance sheets and tougher business conditions lead some financial institutions into *insolvency*, in which net worth becomes negative. Some banks go out of business, and if severe enough, this can lead to a *bank panic*, in which multiple banks fail simultaneously. Because of **asymmetric information**, depositors, who cannot tell the health of the banking system, withdraw their deposits, causing a **bank run**. 

Furthermore, increasingly severe adverse selection and moral hazard problems in financial markets deepen the financial crisis, causing a decline in asset prices and the failure of companies. 

Eventually, authorities shut down and sell insolvent firms. Uncertainty in financial markets declines, the stock market recovers, and balance sheets improve. 

### Stage Three: Debt Deflation 
However, if the economic downturn leads to a sharp decline in the price level, *debt deflation* occurs. An **unanticipated decline in the price level raises the value of borrowing firms' liabilities** in real terms but not the value of their assets. 

The substantial decline in the real net worth of borrowers from a sharp drop in the price level causes an increase in adverse selection and moral hazard problems. Lending and economic activity decline for a long time. 

### The Great Depression 
The most significant financial crisis that displayed debt deflation was the Great Depression. 

In 1928 and 1929, prices doubled in the U.S. stock market. The Fed pursued a tightening of monetary policy to raise interest rates to limit the rise in stock prices. Stock prices crashed in Oct. 1929 and fell by 40% by the end of 1929. 

In 1930, severe droughts in the Midwest led to defaults on farm mortgages, causing large loan losses on bank balance sheets. It prompted substantial withdrawals from banks, building to a full-fledged panic in Dec. 1930. More than one-third of U.S. commercial banks had failed. 

Stock prices kept falling: by mid-1932, stocks had declined to 10% of their value at the 1929 peak. With a greatly reduced number of financial intermediaries, adverse selection and moral hazard problems intensified further. The credit spread rose. 

The price level declined by over 25% and short-circuited the normal recovery process. The huge decline in prices triggered a debt deflation in which net worth fell. The decline in net worth and the resulting increase in adverse selection and moral hazard problems in the credit markets led to a prolonged economic contraction in which unemployment rose to 25%. 

### 2007-2009 Global Financial Crisis

#### Causes of the 2007-2009 Financial Crisis
Before 2000, only prime borrowers could obtain residential mortgages. With computer technology and credit-scoring systems, banks started to offer subprime mortgages and bundled them into standardized debt securities called *mortgage-backed securities*. **Financial innovation did not stop there** and created products like collateralized debt obligations (CDOs). 

The **mortgage brokers** that originated the loans often did not make a strong effort to evaluate the default risk of borrowers, since **they would quickly sell the loans to investors in the form of mortgage-backed securities** (principal-agent problems). 

**Commercial and investment banks**, which underwrote mortgage-backed securities and CDOs, also had weak incentives to check for risk. 

**Rating agencies** advised clients on how to structure complex financial instruments while at the same time rating those products. They also had insufficient incentives to make sure the ratings were accurate. 

Adverse selection became a problem, as investors obtained loans that would be very profitable if housing prices went up. 

#### Effects of the 2007-2009 Financial Crisis
The housing price boom started after the 2001 recession. With high housing prices, even the subprime borrowers could refinance their houses with larger loans when the homes appreciated in value. With housing prices rising, subprime borrowers were also unlikely to default because they could always sell the house to pay off the loan. 

With that, the underwriting standards for subprime mortgages fell lower and lower. Eventually, the housing price bubble burst, causing the value of houses to fall below the amount of their mortgages. When that happened, many struggling homeowners had incentives to walk away from their homes and default on their mortgages. 

The decline in U.S. housing prices led to a decline in financial companies' net worth. The impact even forced major players in financial markets to take drastic action. In March 2008, Bear Stearns, the fifth-largest investment bank in the U.S., was forced to sell itself to J.P. Morgan. On Sep. 15, 2008, after suffering losses in the subprime market, Lehman Brothers, the fourth-largest investment bank by asset size, filed for bankruptcy, making it the largest bankruptcy filing in U.S. history. The day before, Merrill Lynch, the third-largest investment bank, announced its sale to Bank of America. On Sep. 16, 2008, AIG, an insurance giant with assets over $1 trillion, suffered an extreme liquidity crisis. 

## Dynamics of Financial Crises in Emerging Market Economies
In emerging market economies, the dynamics of financial crises have some differences. 

![Sequence of events in financial crises in emerging market economies](/images/money-and-banking/fc-stages-of-a-financial-crisis-emerging-markets.png)
*Sequence of Events in Financial Crises in Emerging Market Economies*

### Stage One: Initiation of Financial Crisis 
In emerging market countries, financial crises develop along two basic paths. 

#### Path A: Mismanagement of Financial Liberalization/Globalization 
The seeds of a financial crisis in emerging market countries are often sown when countries liberalize their financial systems. Lending booms that accompany financial liberalization are typically marked by risky lending practices. Banks pay high interest rates to attract foreign capital and can rapidly increase their lending. 

As in advanced countries, the lending boom ends in a lending crash. Significant loan losses weaken bank balance sheets and prompt banks to cut back on lending. 

The lending crash is not inevitable, especially with prudential regulation and supervision to limit excessive risk. However, due to the principal-agent problem, powerful business interests will want to prevent the supervisors from doing their jobs. 

#### Path B: Severe Fiscal Imbalance 
The financing of government spending can cause a financial crisis as well. When governments face large fiscal imbalances and cannot finance their debt, they often force banks to purchase government debt. Investors who lost confidence in the ability of the government to repay their debt sell the bonds, causing bond prices to plummet. Banks that hold the debt face a huge decline in their net worth. With less capital, the institutions will cut back on lending. **The result of severe fiscal imbalances is a weakening of the banking system**. 

### Stage Two: Currency Crisis 
With all that happened in Stage One, the country's currency will be subjected to a **speculative attack**, in which speculators engage in massive sales of the currency. As currency sales flood the market, supply far outstrips demand, and the value of the currency collapses. 

#### Deterioration of Bank Balance Sheets 
When banks are in trouble, governments have few options for defending the currency other than **raising interest rates**. If the government increases the interest rates, banks must pay more to obtain funds, which **decreases their profitability and can lead to insolvency**. 

Speculators recognize the troubles and bet on the government to give up defending the currency. Once the country's central bank has exhausted its holdings of foreign currency reserves, the cycle ends. 

#### Severe Fiscal Imbalances
When government budget deficits spin out of control, foreign and domestic investors begin to doubt the government's ability to repay the debt and start pulling money out of the country and selling the domestic currency. 

### Stage Three: Full-Fledged Financial Crisis
Emerging market economies, unlike most advanced economies, have many debt contracts in foreign currency, while their assets are denominated in domestic currency, leading to a currency mismatch. 

An unanticipated depreciation of the domestic currency in emerging market countries increases the debt burden in terms of domestic currency. Since companies' assets are in domestic currency, their net worth declines, which increases the adverse selection and moral hazard problems. As a result, it leads to a full-fledged financial crisis.
