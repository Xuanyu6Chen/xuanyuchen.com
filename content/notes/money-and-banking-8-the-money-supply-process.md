As we have seen in [[money-and-banking-4-the-behavior-of-interest-rates|The Behavior of Interest Rates]], **movements in the money supply affect interest rates and inflation**. 

From [[money-and-banking-2-what-is-money|What Is Money]], we know that the money supply can be measured as **M1 and M2**. There are three players in the money supply process: the central bank, banks, and depositors. 

Of the three, the central bank, [[money-and-banking-7-central-banks-and-the-federal-reserve-system|the Fed]], is the most important. It influences the money supply indirectly, through the conduct of monetary policy. 

## The Fed's Balance Sheet
Just like a company, the Fed has its own *balance sheet*, but its items are different. The Fed's balance sheet is very important for understanding monetary policy. 

Let's look at a simplified version.

| Assets | Liabilities |
|---|---|
| Securities | Currency in circulation |
| Loans to financial institutions | Reserves |

### Liabilities 
There are two liabilities on the balance sheet, *currency in circulation and reserves*. An increase in either or both raises the monetary base. 

#### Currency in circulation 
**The Fed issues currency**. Currency in circulation is *the amount of currency in the hands of the public*. 

#### Reserves
All banks have an account at the Fed in which they hold deposits. *Reserves consist of deposits at the Fed plus currency that is physically held by banks*. Reserves are assets for banks but liabilities of the Fed. 

Required reserves (which the Fed requires banks to hold) + excess reserves (any additional reserves that banks choose to hold) = total reserves. 

Since March 2020, the reserve requirement has been 0%, so *all reserves are now excess reserves*. 

### Assets 
The two assets on the Fed's balance sheet can change the money supply and earn income. 

#### Securities 
This category *covers the Fed's holdings of securities issued by the U.S. Treasury*. 

By purchasing securities, the Fed provides reserves to the banks. 

#### Loans to financial institutions
The second way the Fed can provide reserves to the banking system is *by making loans to banks and other financial institutions*. 

The interest rate charged to banks for these loans is called the *discount rate*, one of the three policy tools that the Fed uses. 

## The Monetary Base
The sum of the *Fed's monetary liabilities* (currency in circulation and reserves) and *the U.S. Treasury's monetary liabilities* (Treasury currency in circulation, primarily coins) is called **the monetary base**.

The monetary base (high-powered money) equals currency in circulation C plus the total reserves in the banking system, R.

$$ MB = C + R $$

**The Fed can directly influence the monetary base, which affects how many reserves are in the banking system, and so it indirectly influences the money supply.** 

### Open Market Operations 
The primary way to control the monetary base is through *open market operations*. 

#### Open Market Purchase from a Bank
The Fed purchases $100 million of bonds from banks. The banking system swaps $100 million of securities for $100 million of reserves, both on the asset side. The Fed has an additional $100 million of reserves in its liabilities. 

| | Assets | Liabilities |
|---|---|---|
| **Banking system** | Securities −\$100m · Reserves +\$100m | |
| **The Fed** | Securities +\$100m | Reserves +\$100m |

**Result:** 
	R +100 · C 0 · MB +100
	**Money supply:** M 0 (reserves are not part of M)

#### Open Market Purchase from the Nonbank Public
The Fed purchases $100 million of bonds from people or corporations. The nonbank public receives a $100 million check from the Fed for the bonds. 

##### Case 1: Depositing the Check
If the nonbank public deposits the check, the banking system gains $100 million in reserves, which increases the Fed's liabilities by the same amount. 

| | Assets | Liabilities |
|---|---|---|
| **Nonbank public** | Securities −\$100m · Checkable deposits +\$100m | |
| **Banking system** | Reserves +\$100m | Checkable deposits +\$100m |
| **The Fed** | Securities +\$100m | Reserves +\$100m |

**Result:** 
	R +100 · C 0 · MB +100
	**Money supply:** M +100 (new deposits)

##### Case 2: Cashing the Check
If the nonbank public cashes the check, the Fed has an additional $100 million of currency in circulation. 

| | Assets | Liabilities |
|---|---|---|
| **Nonbank public** | Securities −\$100m · Currency +\$100m | |
| **The Fed** | Securities +\$100m | Currency in circulation +\$100m |

**Result:** 
	R 0 · C +100 · MB +100
	**Money supply:** M +100 (new currency held by the public)

#### Open Market Sale
The Fed sells $100 million of bonds to banks or the nonbank public.

##### Banks 

| | Assets | Liabilities |
|---|---|---|
| **Banking system** | Securities +\$100m · Reserves −\$100m | |
| **The Fed** | Securities −\$100m | Reserves −\$100m |

**Result:** 
	R −100 · C 0 · MB −100
	**Money supply:** M 0 (the bank only swapped reserves for bonds)

##### Nonbank Public 

| | Assets | Liabilities |
|---|---|---|
| **Nonbank public** | Securities +\$100m · Currency −\$100m | |
| **The Fed** | Securities −\$100m | Currency in circulation −\$100m |

**Result:** 
	R 0 · C −100 · MB −100
	**Money supply:** M −100 (currency leaves the public)

### Shifts from Deposits into Currency
Depositors withdraw $100 million of currency from banks. The banking system loses $100 million of reserves, but the Fed loses nothing: its liabilities just shift from reserves to currency in circulation. 

| | Assets | Liabilities |
|---|---|---|
| **Nonbank public** | Checkable deposits −\$100m · Currency +\$100m | |
| **Banking system** | Reserves −\$100m | Checkable deposits −\$100m |
| **The Fed** | | Currency in circulation +\$100m · Reserves −\$100m |

**Result:** 
	R −100 · C +100 · MB 0
	**Money supply:** M 0 (deposits became currency; both are in M)

### Discount Loans
The Fed lends $100 million to a bank. The Fed's assets increase by $100 million (discount loans), and its liabilities increase by $100 million (reserves). 

| | Assets | Liabilities |
|---|---|---|
| **Banking system** | Reserves +\$100m | Discount loans (borrowings from the Fed) +\$100m |
| **The Fed** | Discount loans +\$100m | Reserves +\$100m |

**Result:** 
	R +100 · C 0 · MB +100
	**Money supply:** M 0 (until the bank lends the reserves out)

The bank pays back the $100 million loan. 

| | Assets | Liabilities |
|---|---|---|
| **Banking system** | Reserves −\$100m | Discount loans (borrowings from the Fed) −\$100m |
| **The Fed** | Discount loans −\$100m | Reserves −\$100m |

**Result:** 
	R −100 · C 0 · MB −100
	**Money supply:** M 0

### The Fed's Ability to Control the Monetary Base
We can split the monetary base into two components: one that the Fed completely controls, which we call the *nonborrowed monetary base*, and another that is less tightly controlled. 

$$ MB_n = MB - BR $$
where $MB_{n}$ = nonborrowed monetary base; BR = borrowed reserves from the Fed. 

## The Money Multiplier
When the Fed supplies the banking system with $1 of additional reserves, deposits increase by a multiple of the amount. Let's first use a simple model to understand why. 

### Multiple Deposit Creation (Simple Model)
Suppose the *required reserve ratio (rr)*, one of the three policy tools, is set at 10% for all banks, and no banks hold excess reserves.

The Fed conducts an open market purchase of $100 million in bonds from the nonbank public, who deposit the Fed's check in Bank A. 

With the 10% reserve requirement, Bank A keeps $10 million as reserves and **can make $90 million of loans**. Bank A's *T-account* (which lists only the changes in its balance sheet items) will look like this:

| Bank A                  | Assets                         | Liabilities                |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------ | -------------------------- |
| **Deposit arrives**     | Reserves +\$100m               | Checkable deposits +\$100m |
| **After lending \$90m** | Reserves +\$10m · Loans +\$90m | Checkable deposits +\$100m |

Suppose all the money spent by the borrowers is deposited in Bank B. With the reserve requirement, Bank B **can make loans totaling $81 million to borrowers**.

| Bank B                  | Assets                        | Liabilities               |
| ----------------------- | ----------------------------- | ------------------------- |
| **Deposit arrives**     | Reserves +\$90m               | Checkable deposits +\$90m |
| **After lending \$81m** | Reserves +\$9m · Loans +\$81m | Checkable deposits +\$90m |

The $81 million spent by borrowers from Bank B will be deposited in Bank C. Following the same reasoning, the total increase in deposits **from the initial $100 million increase in reserves will be $1,000 million**. 

| Bank | Increase in Deposits (\$m) | Increase in Loans (\$m) | Increase in Reserves (\$m) |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 100.00 | 90.00 | 10.00 |
| B | 90.00 | 81.00 | 9.00 |
| C | 81.00 | 72.90 | 8.10 |
| D | 72.90 | 65.61 | 7.29 |
| E | 65.61 | 59.05 | 6.56 |
| F | 59.05 | 53.14 | 5.90 |
| ⋮ | ⋮ | ⋮ | ⋮ |
| **Total for all banks** | **1,000.00** | **900.00** | **100.00** |

We call this multiple the *simple deposit multiplier*. 

$$ \Delta D = \frac{1}{rr} \times \Delta R $$

where $\Delta D$ = change in total deposits; $rr$ = required reserve ratio; $\Delta R$ = change in reserves

### Factors That Determine the Money Supply
**Money Supply Response** (holding all other factors constant)

| Player | Variable | Change | Money supply | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| The Fed | Nonborrowed monetary base, $MB_n$ | ↑ | ↑ | More MB, so more loans and deposits |
| The Fed | Required reserve ratio, $rr$ | ↑ | ↓ | Less multiple deposit expansion |
| Banks | Borrowed reserves, $BR$ | ↑ | ↑ | More MB, so more loans and deposits |
| Banks | Excess reserves ratio, $e$ | ↑ | ↓ | Fewer loans, so less deposit creation |
| Depositors | Currency ratio, $c$ | ↑ | ↓ | Currency is not lent out, so less multiple deposit expansion |

### The Money Multiplier Formula
The simple model only considers the required reserve ratio but doesn't take excess reserves or currency holdings into account. 

The money multiplier m finally tells us what multiple of the *monetary base (MB) is transformed into the money supply*. 

$$ M = m \times MB, \qquad m = \frac{1 + c}{rr + e + c} $$
where $m$ = money multiplier; $c = C/D$ = currency ratio; $e = ER/D$ = excess reserves ratio; $rr$ = required reserve ratio


$$ MB = C + R = c \times D + rr \times D + e \times D = (rr + e + c) \times D $$

$$ D = \frac{1}{rr + e + c} \times MB $$

$$ M = D + C = (1 + c) \times D = \frac{1 + c}{rr + e + c} \times MB $$

## 2008 Financial Crisis: Quantitative Easing and the Money Supply
After the 2008 financial crisis, the Fed conducted **quantitative easing (QE)**, buying large amounts of bonds through open market purchases. The **monetary base rose about fivefold**, but **M1 only doubled**, far less than the money multiplier would suggest. Banks held most of the new reserves instead of lending them, so the excess reserves ratio $e$ rose and the multiplier fell.

| | Jul 2007 | Aug 2014 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monetary base (MB) | \$829b | \$4,075b | × 4.9 |
| M1 | \$1,372b | \$2,803b | × 2.0 |
| M1 multiplier ($M1/MB$) | 1.65 | 0.69 | −58% |

Source: FRED series BOGMBASE, M1SL (monthly).
