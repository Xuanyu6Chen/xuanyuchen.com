Banking plays an important role in channeling funds to borrowers with productive investment opportunities. Banks provide loans to businesses, finance our college educations and purchases of a car or a house, and provide us with services like checking accounts, savings accounts, debit cards, and ATMs. 

Let's take a look at how banks manage their money to earn the highest profits while reducing risk. 

## The Bank Balance Sheet 
Banks have the same balance sheet equation as other companies -- **total assets = total liabilities + capital**. 

![Balance sheet of all U.S. commercial banks, June 2026](/images/money-and-banking/bk-bank-balance-sheet.png)
*Balance Sheet of All U.S. Commercial Banks, June 2026*

### Liabilities 
**A bank acquires funds by issuing liabilities**, such as deposits. 

#### Checkable Deposits 
*Checkable deposits* are bank accounts that allow the owner of the account to *write checks to third parties*. The **interest paid** on checkable deposits may differ by type, such as non-interest-bearing accounts, interest-bearing NOW accounts, and money market deposit accounts.

In June 2026, checkable deposits (excluding MMDAs, as in FDIC data) accounted for around 32% of total bank assets (total liabilities + capital). 

#### Nontransaction Deposits 
*Nontransaction deposits* are the primary source of bank funds, around 40% of total bank assets in June 2026 (including MMDAs, as in FDIC data). Owners generally **cannot write checks** on nontransaction deposits, but the **interest rates paid are usually higher** than those on checkable deposits. 

Two basic types of nontransaction deposits are savings accounts and time deposits (CDs). 

#### Borrowings 
Banks also obtain funds **by borrowing** from the Federal Reserve System and other lenders. 

Borrowings from the Fed are called *discount loans* ([[money-and-banking-9-tools-of-monetary-policy#Discount Policy|Discount Policy]]). Banks also borrow reserves overnight in the *federal funds market* from other U.S. banks and financial institutions. In June 2026, borrowings made up around 7.4% of total bank assets. 

#### Bank Capital 
The final category on the liabilities side is bank capital, the **bank's net worth**. 

**Bank capital is raised by selling new equity or retaining earnings**. It is a cushion against a drop in the value of the bank's assets. 

### Assets 
A bank uses the funds that it has acquired by issuing liabilities **to purchase income-earning assets**. 

#### Reserves 
Reserves are banks' deposits at the Fed plus currency that is physically held by banks. Reserves required by the Fed are called *required reserves*, and the rest are called *excess reserves*. 

In March 2020, the Fed introduced a 0% requirement, so 100% of the reserves have been excess reserves since then. In June 2026, excess reserves at commercial banks were about 6.5% of total assets. 

#### Cash Items in Process of Collection 
Suppose a check written on an account at Bank B is deposited in Bank A, and the funds have not yet been received from Bank B. The check is classified as *a cash item in process of collection*, and it is an asset for Bank A. 

#### Deposits at Other Banks 
Many small banks hold deposits at larger banks in exchange for a variety of services, an arrangement called correspondent banking. 

#### Securities 
A bank's holdings of securities are **an important income-earning asset**. Banks mostly hold government agency and Treasury securities, along with some municipal bonds and other securities. 

#### Loans 
Banks **make their profits primarily by issuing loans**. A loan is a liability for the individual or corporation receiving it, but an asset for the bank. 

The largest categories of loans for commercial banks are real estate loans and *commercial and industrial loans* made to businesses. 

## Basic Banking 
Let's use a *T-account*, a simplified balance sheet listing only the changes, to show some basic banking transactions. 

### Deposit
![A $100 cash deposit](/images/money-and-banking/bk-t-account-cash-deposit.png)

When we deposit \$100 in cash into Bank A, checkable deposits increase by \$100 on the liabilities side, along with a \$100 increase in reserves on the assets side. 

### Check 
![A new account opened with a $100 check](/images/money-and-banking/bk-t-account-check-deposit.png)

When we deposit a \$100 check written on an account at another bank, Bank B, checkable deposits increase by \$100, and Bank A is owed \$100 by Bank B.

The \$100 first enters Bank A's assets as cash items in process of collection, and it turns into reserves after the check clears and Bank A receives the funds from Bank B. 

### Reserves into Loans
![Turning reserves into loans](/images/money-and-banking/bk-t-account-reserves-to-loans.png)

Because reserves usually earn less than loans (and vault cash earns no interest), Bank A may take a loss after servicing costs if it holds all the money as reserves. It is even worse if the bank pays interest on the deposits. 

To earn more, Bank A can invest in securities or make loans. It can use its excess reserves (total reserves minus required reserves) to make loans. 

## General Principles of Bank Management 
Bank management is about how a bank manages its assets and liabilities to earn the highest possible profit. There are four primary concerns. 

First, to keep enough cash on hand for deposit outflows, the bank must engage in **liquidity management**. Second, the bank must pursue a low level of risk by acquiring assets that have a low rate of default and by diversifying asset holdings, which is called **asset management**. Third, the bank must acquire funds at a low cost, which is called **liability management**. Fourth, the bank must decide how much capital it should maintain, which is called **capital adequacy management**. 

Let's look at each one of them in detail. 

### Liquidity Management 

Let's assume Bank A has \$110 million in total assets and faces a deposit outflow of \$10 million. 

In Case 1, Bank A has \$15 million in reserves, which can cover the entire deposit outflow. In Case 2, however, Bank A has only \$4 million in reserves, so the \$10 million deposit outflow **results in a \$6 million reserve shortfall**.  

![A $10 million deposit outflow at Bank A](/images/money-and-banking/bk-liquidity-deposit-outflow.png)

To eliminate the shortfall, the bank has four basic options. 

One is to *acquire reserves by borrowing from other banks in the federal funds market or by borrowing from corporations*. 

![Borrow from banks or other firms](/images/money-and-banking/bk-liquidity-1-borrow-from-banks.png)

A second alternative is for the bank to *sell some of its securities* to help cover the deposit outflow. 

![Sell securities](/images/money-and-banking/bk-liquidity-2-sell-securities.png)

A third way is to acquire reserves by *borrowing from the Fed*.

![Borrow from the Fed](/images/money-and-banking/bk-liquidity-3-borrow-from-the-fed.png)

Finally, a bank can *reduce its loans* to cover the shortfall, but this is usually the costliest way of acquiring reserves. Banks can either **call in loans** (not renew some loans when they come due) or **sell them off to other banks at a lower price**. 

![Reduce loans](/images/money-and-banking/bk-liquidity-4-reduce-loans.png)

### Asset Management 
To maximize profit, a bank must **simultaneously seek the highest returns possible on loans and securities, reduce risk, and make adequate provisions for liquidity by holding liquid assets**. 

First, banks try to find borrowers who will *pay high interest rates and are unlikely to default*. They seek out loan business by advertising and directly approaching corporations. 

Second, banks try to *purchase securities with high returns and low risk*. 

Third, banks must attempt to *lower risk by diversifying*. They purchase many different types of assets and approve many types of loans. 

### Liability Management 
Before the 1960s, **liabilities determined assets**, and banks spent time trying to achieve an optimal mix of assets. 

Starting in the 1960s, the direction flipped. Banks now find good loans, decide how much money they need, and go out and get the money. A *negotiable CD* is a large time deposit that the holder can sell before it matures. This feature made companies more willing to lend to banks. 

![Liability management: the loan comes first](/images/money-and-banking/bk-t-account-liability-management.png)

### Capital Adequacy Management 
**Bank capital helps prevent bank failure, and the amount of capital affects returns** for the equity holders of the bank. It is important to decide how much capital a bank needs to hold. 

A bank maintains bank capital to lessen the chance that it will become insolvent. **The more capital a bank has, the better it can absorb losses from defaults**. 

#### Returns 

A basic measure of bank profitability is the *return on assets* (ROA):

$$\text{ROA} = \frac{\text{net profit after taxes}}{\text{assets}}$$

Another basic measure of bank profitability is the *return on equity* (ROE):

$$\text{ROE} = \frac{\text{net profit after taxes}}{\text{equity capital}}$$

ROA and ROE are directly related through the *equity multiplier* (EM):

$$\text{EM} = \frac{\text{assets}}{\text{equity capital}}$$

$$\text{ROE} = \text{ROA} \times \text{EM} = \text{ROA} \times \frac{\text{assets}}{\text{equity capital}}$$

From the formulas above, we can see that if equity capital decreases, ROE increases. **Given the return on assets, the lower the bank capital, the higher the return for the owners of the bank**. 

Now, we can see a clear trade-off between safety and returns to equity holders. Bank capital benefits the owners by making the investment safer, but bank capital is also costly. 

In determining the amount of bank capital, banks must decide how much of the increased safety that comes with higher capital they are willing to trade off against the lower return on equity that comes with higher capital. 

## Managing Credit Risk 
Banks must make successful loans that are paid back in full if they are to earn high profits, so managing credit risk is critical. 

The economic concepts of **adverse selection and moral hazard** provide a framework for understanding the principles that financial institutions have to follow to reduce credit risk and make successful loans. 

Adverse selection in loan markets occurs because **bad credit risks are the ones most likely to line up for loans**. They are the least desirable borrowers. Moral hazard exists in loan markets because **borrowers may have incentives to engage in activities that are undesirable** from the lender's point of view. 

To be profitable, banks must overcome the adverse selection and moral hazard problems by following several principles. 

### Screening and Monitoring 
**Asymmetric information** is present in loan markets because lenders have less information about the borrowers than the borrowers themselves do. Therefore, screening is necessary to reduce the adverse selection problem. 

Once a loan has been made, a moral hazard problem may arise that makes the loan less likely to be paid off. To reduce moral hazard, financial institutions must **write provisions (restrictive covenants)** into loan contracts that restrict borrowers from engaging in risky activities. 

### Long-Term Customer Relationships 
If a prospective borrower has had an account or other loans with a bank over a long period of time, a loan officer can look at past activity on the accounts and learn more about the borrower. 

This history **lowers the cost of screening**, so the bank can better judge whether the borrower is a good credit risk. 

Long-term relationships also reduce moral hazard. A borrower who wants to keep borrowing from the bank in the future has a reason to **avoid risky activities** that would upset the bank. Monitoring is also cheaper, because the bank already knows the borrower well. 

### Loan Commitments 
A *loan commitment* is a bank's promise to lend a firm up to a set amount over a set period, usually at an interest rate tied to a market rate. 

The firm benefits because it **has credit available whenever it needs it**. The bank benefits because the commitment **builds a long-term relationship**. In return, the firm usually has to keep giving the bank information about its income, assets and liabilities, and business activities, which makes screening and monitoring easier. 

### Collateral
*Collateral* is property pledged to the lender, which the lender can take if the borrower defaults. It **reduces the lender's loss** when a loan is not paid back. 

A special form of collateral is a *compensating balance*, a minimum amount that a firm receiving a loan must keep in a checking account at the bank. If the borrower defaults, the bank can take the balance. The account also **helps the bank monitor the borrower**. 

### Credit Rationing 
*Credit rationing* means that lenders refuse to make loans even though borrowers are willing to pay the stated interest rate or even a higher one. It takes two forms. 

First, a bank may **refuse to lend at all**, even at a higher rate. Borrowers with very risky projects are the most willing to pay high rates. Raising the rate would attract worse borrowers, which is the adverse selection problem. 

Second, a bank may **lend less than the borrower wants**. A larger loan increases the borrower's incentive to engage in risky activities, which is the moral hazard problem. A smaller loan keeps the borrower's gain from taking risks small. 

## Managing Interest-Rate Risk 
Banks and other financial institutions have become more concerned about their exposure to *interest-rate risk*. **If a bank has more rate-sensitive liabilities than assets, a rise in interest rates will reduce bank profits, and a decline in interest rates will raise bank profits**. 

Interest-rate risk is how much a bank could lose when rates change. Let's look at the two most common analyses for measuring it. 

### Gap Analysis 
*Gap analysis* measures how a change in interest rates affects a bank's **income**. The bank subtracts its rate-sensitive liabilities (RSL) from its rate-sensitive assets (RSA). 

$$\text{GAP} = \text{RSA} - \text{RSL}$$

Multiplying the gap by the change in interest rates gives the change in bank income: 

$$\Delta \text{Income} = \text{GAP} \times \Delta i$$

For example, Bank A has \$20 million in rate-sensitive assets and \$50 million in rate-sensitive liabilities, so its gap is −\$30 million. If interest rates rise by 5 percentage points, its income falls by \$30 million × 5% = \$1.5 million. 

**With a negative gap, rising interest rates reduce income**. With a positive gap, rising interest rates increase income. Gap analysis only looks at income, so it ignores how rate changes affect the **market value** of assets and liabilities. 

### Duration Analysis 
*Duration analysis* measures how a change in interest rates affects a bank's **net worth**. *Duration* measures how sensitive a security's value is to interest rates. The bank compares the duration of its assets ($D_A$) with that of its liabilities ($D_L$), where $L/A$ is liabilities over assets: 

$$\text{DUR}_{\text{gap}} = D_A - \frac{L}{A} \times D_L$$

$$\frac{\Delta \text{NW}}{A} \approx -\text{DUR}_{\text{gap}} \times \Delta i$$

For example, with a duration gap of 2.1 years, a 2-percentage-point rise in interest rates cuts net worth by about 4.2% of assets. 

**With a positive duration gap, rising interest rates reduce a bank's net worth**. 

## Off-Balance-Sheet Activities 
Off-balance-sheet activities have grown in importance for banks. 

### Loan Sales 
A *loan sale* involves a contract that sells all or part of the cash stream from a specific loan and thereby removes the loan from the bank's balance sheet. **Banks earn profits by selling loans for slightly more than the original loan amount**. 

### Generation of Fee Income 
Banks also earn *fee income* by providing services to customers. These services do not add assets or liabilities to the balance sheet. Some examples: 
- **Foreign exchange** trades for customers. 
- **Servicing** mortgage-backed securities: collecting the interest and principal payments and passing them to the investors. 
- **Backup lines of credit**, such as loan commitments and overdraft privileges, where customers pay for the right to borrow later.
